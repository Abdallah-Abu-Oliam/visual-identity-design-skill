Visual language · five

Le Grain

Three meanings in one word — the seed, the surface texture of a material, and the wheat already standing in the mark. The same trick as Noyau itself.

Why this exists at all. The assembled identity failed the boring test: the mark was unmistakable and everything around it could have belonged to a skincare company. Worse, at a festival, at distance, the surfaces read as nothing until you were close enough to see the mark. A logo, a palette and a typeface are each one decision a competitor copies in an afternoon. A pattern taken from the brand's own material cannot be taken back without taking the brand.

Where it comes from

Not decoration applied afterwards. The question was what is this brand physically made of — and a noyau is the stone at the centre of a fruit, whose surface is grooved, pitted and ridged. That texture belongs to the name.

Construction

TileSeamless 400 × 400, wrapped on both axes
Marks150, placed by toroidal Poisson sampling — never closer than 22 units, measured across the boundary too, so density stays even
DirectionAngles from a flow field, sin(x/118) + cos(y/96) — this is what makes the grain run instead of scattering like confetti
VariationLengths 9–30 at near-constant width. 16% are round pits, which is what stops it reading as fur
ColourOne flat fill. No strokes, no gradients — photocopy-safe

Three registers, one tile

The tile never changes; scale is the variable. The large register is the one that solves the failure — at forty metres nobody reads a logo, they see a surface.

The argument — before and after

Same mark, same colours, same type. The only difference is Le Grain.

The content rule

Content sits on flat colour. Always. Where text must appear over Le Grain, a solid plate of Paper or Ink goes underneath it — never a transparency. Le Grain appears where it stands alone: panel backgrounds, card reverses, banner fields, the margins of a spread, endpapers.
A pattern that competes with the message has cost more than it gained.
The mark needs a plate too — not just the text. Its wreath separations are fine negative space, engineered by boolean subtraction. Against a texture they fill in and the wreath turns into a blob. Look at the banner above: the mark sits on its own small solid block, not on the grain.
This rule was broken on this book's own cover in the first draft — the mark and the body copy were both laid straight onto the pattern, and the copy was unreadable. Recorded because it is the easiest rule here to break by accident, and because a guideline that contradicts itself teaches people to ignore all of it.

Cropping

Never rotate the tile. The flow field has a direction; two rotated applications will visibly disagree.
Never scale below ~80px or above ~1400px per tile. Below, it turns to noise; above, the individual marks read as objects and it stops being texture.

Contrast ceiling

Le Grain is a tone, not a graphic. It must stay quiet against whatever it sits on.

Sand grain on PaperIdeal — the default
Apricot grain on PaperWarm accent panels
Paper grain on Ink or TerracottaReversed panels
Ink grain on Paper⚠️ Large register only — too loud when fine
Apricot grain on SandToo close in value — turns to mud
A coarser variant was made and rejected. Long curved strokes, sparser and heavier. It read as scribble and brambles — busy, aggressive, hand-drawn in the wrong way — and failed the personality directly, which is set at reserved and indirect. Recorded so it is not re-proposed: it was built, rendered, looked at, and turned down.
Used once, a pattern is an ornament. Used everywhere, it becomes the thing people recognise before they read the name. That is the only reason this element exists, and it only pays off if it is applied consistently.
