# Phase 1 — The Brief

Seven elements. **Facts the owner already has** — this is capture, not creative work. The
single exception is the name, which has a method when it does not yet exist.

Each element below carries three things: what it **means**, how its answer is
**processed** into visual decisions, and the **right question** to get it.

**Two rules across all seven.**

*Ask the question as written, not the label.* "What is your target audience?" returns
"everyone." The questions below are phrased to make that answer impossible.

*Push back once, then move on.* A vague answer gets one sharper follow-up. Take whatever
comes back and continue. This is a brief, not an interrogation, and the questionnaire and
mood board both get further chances at it.

---

## 1 · Quick tour — what it does

**Meaning.** What the thing actually does, in plain language. Not the pitch, not the
mission — the mechanics. What a customer encounters and what happens next.

**Processing.** Establishes the **category**, and category rules whole visual territories
in or out before taste enters. It also enumerates the **surfaces**: an app needs an icon
that survives 16px and a UI-capable palette; a shop needs signage, packaging, and a
one-colour mark for engraving. Surfaces determine what actually gets built, and they
cross-check the answer to Q13 later.

**The right question.**
> "Walk me through it like I am a customer seeing it for the first time. What is it, and
> what happens?"

**Failure mode.** The investor pitch — *"we are a platform revolutionising…"*. That
describes ambition, not mechanics, and it names no category and no surfaces. Push once:
*"What does someone actually do with it, step by step?"*

## 2 · Name

**Meaning.** The word that will be drawn.

**Processing.**

- **Length** decides wordmark viability. Long names cannot carry a wordmark; they push
  toward lettermark or combination.
- **Script and language** decide how many times it gets drawn. Bilingual means two
  wordmarks, designed separately, locking to one symbol — never one translated at the end.
- **Whether the word is already an object** decides whether the symbol can come from the
  name itself. النصال means blades; that is a gift, and the mark can come straight out of
  the word. An invented word gives nothing and the symbol must come from elsewhere.
- **Sound and rhythm** inform letterform character — hard consonants suit sharper forms.

**The right question.**
> "What is it called? Spell it exactly as it should appear, capitalisation and all. And
> is that final, or still provisional?"

The provisional check matters. Designing a wordmark around a name that changes wastes the
most expensive work in the process.

**If there is no name yet**, do not ask again — run the naming method:
https://stripe.com/resources/more/how-to-pick-a-name-for-your-startup-a-step-by-step-guide

**Always check what the name collides with.** One Behance search for `knife brand
identity` surfaced *"Nesal"* — a published knife identity under a near-identical name.
Trademark exposure and visual collision are both real, and both are cheap to check now
and expensive to discover later.

## 3 · Reason for the name

**Meaning.** Why that word and not another.

**Processing.** **The richest source of symbol ideas in the entire brief.** Meaning
becomes metaphor becomes drawable form. It also classifies the name, which changes how
much work the logo has to do:

| Name type | Consequence |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Descriptive** — says what it is | Logo is free to be abstract; the name already explains |
| **Metaphorical** — النصال, Jamrah | Logo can extend the metaphor; strongest case |
| **Arbitrary** — invented, or "it sounded good" | Logo carries all the meaning alone; the symbol must come from values or category instead |

**The right question.**
> "Why that word? What does it mean — and what made you pick it over the alternatives you
> considered?"

The second half is the valuable half. What was rejected reveals the territory the owner
has already ruled out, usually without realising they were making a design decision.

**Watch for competing metaphors.** Al-Nissal's brief carried two — blades *and* a falcon.
Two metaphors in one mark usually means neither lands. Flag it here; resolve it on the
mind map, where عين الصقر and الحدة turned out to be one form rather than two.

## 4 · Targeted audience

**Meaning.** Who this is actually for.

**Processing.**

- **Age and viewing context** set legibility floors — type size, contrast, density.
- **Culture and region** set colour meaning, script direction, and symbol taboos. The
  colour meanings table is one culture's reading; it must be read against this answer.
- **Expertise** sets how much the identity explains versus assumes. Professionals want
  specifications; newcomers want reassurance.
- **Professional versus consumer** sets register.

**The right question.**
> "Who would be genuinely disappointed if this disappeared tomorrow?"

Asking "who is your audience" returns a demographic bracket. Asking who would *miss* it
returns actual people.

**Failure mode.** "Everyone." Push once:
> "Pick the one person who would be most annoyed. Who are they, and what were they doing
> when they needed you?"

## 5 · Competitors

**Meaning.** Who else is in the room when someone chooses.

**Processing.** **The differentiation input, and the one that decides the palette.** Full
method in `SPEC.md` under Phase 2 Step 5:

1. Collect their marks and sites.
2. Map every axis — hue, saturation, value, type classification, weight, composition,
   texture, and **volume**.
3. Find the cluster.
4. Name the empty space.
5. Filter that space through brand strategy, deleting whatever strategy forbids.

**Differentiate on register before hue.** Hue is the most visible axis and the easiest to
copy — a competitor changes a hex code in an afternoon. Volume, texture, and composition
are usually emptier and far harder to take back.

**The right question.**
> "Who does a customer seriously consider before choosing you? Name three — and include
> the local ones."

Local matters. The global category leader is rarely who they actually lose sales to, and
a local rival's palette is far more likely to be genuinely occupied in the market that
counts.

**Failure mode.** "We have no competitors." Never true. Reframe:
> "What do people do instead of using you?"

The alternative is always something, even if it is a spreadsheet or doing nothing.

## 6 · Competitive advantages

**Meaning.** Why someone picks them over the names just given.

**Processing.** Decides **what the identity must make felt first** — the one quality the
visual has to carry loudest. Craft as an advantage demands an identity that feels crafted:
material, detail, restraint. Speed demands one that feels efficient: economical, direct,
uncluttered.

There is a second reading. **When the advantage is invisible** — better support, honest
advice, reliable warranty — the identity has to carry more weight, because nothing about
the product demonstrates it on a shelf. Invisible advantages need the visual to signal
trustworthiness that the product cannot show by itself.

**The right question.**
> "Why does someone choose you over the ones you just named? Not what you are good at —
> what makes them switch."

**Failure mode.** Generic virtues: *"quality, service, price."* Every competitor claims
all three. Push once:
> "What can you do that they genuinely cannot?"

---

## 7 · Targeted feelings

**Meaning.** What a person should feel on contact.

**Processing.** **The main bridge from strategy to visual, and the most direct translation
that exists.** Feeling maps almost mechanically:

| Feeling | Drives |
|---------|--------|
| Calm, controlled | Muted palette, generous whitespace, soft radii, low contrast |
| Urgent, energetic | Saturated colour, tight spacing, high contrast, angular forms |
| Premium, considered | Restraint, material texture, wide margins, few elements |
| Warm, approachable | Rounded forms, warm neutrals, human scale |

Most brand processes derive feeling downstream, as an output of good design. Capturing it
here as an **input** is sharper — it gives the equation a variable that would otherwise
stay implicit.

**The right question.**
> "Someone uses this and it goes well. What do they feel in that exact moment? Give me a
> sentence, not adjectives."

**Failure mode.** Generic adjectives — *"trustworthy, modern"* — which fit eighty percent
of all software and drive nothing. Push once with the sentence frame:
> "Trustworthy like a bank, or like a friend who always shows up?"

Compare: `"trustworthy, modern"` decides nothing. `"the relief of finally being
organised"` decides the palette, the spacing, the radii, and rules out red.

**The collision check runs here.** A stated feeling can contradict the audience or the
category — *bold* for sixty-year-old accountants in a category of navy competitors.
Because this skill takes feeling as an input rather than deriving it, that collision is
possible and must be surfaced. Raise it; do not resolve it silently.

## What the brief feeds

| Element | Consumed by |
|---------|-------------|
| 1 · Quick tour | Category conventions · surface list · cross-check on Q13 |
| 2 · Name | Logo type · wordmark count · trademark check |
| 3 · Naming reason | Symbol territory · mind map's starting nodes |
| 4 · Audience | Legibility floors · cultural colour reading · register |
| 5 · Competitors | Research targets · differentiation · palette whitespace |
| 6 · Advantages | What the identity must say loudest |
| 7 · Feelings | Colour temperature · shape language · weight · spacing |

All seven then seed the questionnaire, where roughly 35 of its 47 answers are drafted from
this material rather than asked cold.
