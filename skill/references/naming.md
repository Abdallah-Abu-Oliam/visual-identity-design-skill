# Naming

Runs **only** when the project has no name yet, or the name is provisional. If a final
name exists, capture it and move on — do not reopen it.

Method from Stripe, "How to pick a name for your startup":
https://stripe.com/resources/more/how-to-pick-a-name-for-your-startup-a-step-by-step-guide

**Do not hand the user eleven steps and ask them to work through it.** They came for help
naming something; a checklist is the same blank page in a different shape. Run the method
*for* them — generate the candidates, apply the filters, and bring back a short list with
the reasoning attached. They react. That is the whole approach of this skill applied to
its hardest question.

---

## The eleven steps

### 1 · Brain dump, then brainstorm
Unfiltered ideation about the business — mission, value proposition, audience, the problem
solved. **No judgement at this stage.** Then group what emerged and look for patterns.

*We already have this.* Brief elements 1, 4, 6 and 7 are exactly the raw material. Start
from them rather than asking again.

### 2 · Expose yourself to sources of inspiration
Widen the input: other industries' naming conventions, other languages, art, culture,
domain browsing. Record everything.

*Our version:* pull vocabulary from the brief's own world — materials, processes, tools,
places, and the language the audience actually uses.

### 3 · Two separate lists

| List | What goes on it |
|------|-----------------|
| **Names with a story** | Tied to origin, mission, a proposition, or an anecdote. They carry depth that can be explained |
| **Names that resonate** | Emotionally right without an explicit narrative. Intuitive rather than argued |

Not mutually exclusive — the split exists to clarify thinking.

### 4 · Rule out negative associations
- **Other languages** — what does it mean elsewhere? Consult a native speaker for any market that matters.
- **Historical baggage** — controversial events, people, entities.
- **Legal availability** — a first-pass search.

### 5 · Look at the competition
List competitors, study their names, find the differentiation, and check nothing is too
close. **Balance distinction against category legibility** — the name should signal the
industry while standing apart from everyone in it.

*This is the same tension as the visual competitor method: conformity is occasionally
correct, but it is chosen deliberately, never by default.*

### 6 · Trademark search
Search the relevant trademark office — USPTO for the US, and each jurisdiction the brand
will operate in, since laws differ by region. **"A quick Google search isn't enough."**
Recommend a trademark attorney for anything serious.

### 7 · Keep it simple
Easy to pronounce · consistent to spell · short · distinctive without being difficult.
A simple name is remembered, repeated correctly, and found in search.

### 8 · Choose a name that grows with the business
- Avoid tying it to one product, one service, or one city.
- Test it against the five-to-ten-year plan — which the questionnaire's vision section
  captures.
- Check domain availability.
- Avoid trendy slang, which dates.

### 9 · Consider ownability
Trademark · domain (`.com` first) · social handles · SEO — an over-common word gets buried
· **and whether the name translates into a mark at all.** A name that is already a visual
object is the strongest case; an abstract coinage leaves the logo carrying everything.

### 10 · Get outside perspective
Diverse opinions, small surveys, and a check for unintended readings. **What resonates
with the founder may not land with the audience.**

### 11 · Go with your gut
After the analysis: which one resonates, which is presentable to customers and partners,
which feels durable, which feels ownable. *"The most successful business names blend
strategy with emotional resonance."*

## Four mistakes the source names

1. **Failing to future-proof** — a narrow name forces an expensive rebrand later.
2. **Cultural collision** — catchy in one language, offensive in another.
3. **Difficult spelling** — kills word of mouth and search.
4. **Trademark traps** — similarity to an existing mark is a legal risk, and a Google
   search does not clear it.

---

## How to run this in practice

1. **Generate from the brief**, not from nothing. Elements 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7 supply the
   territory. Build both lists — story and resonance — with candidates in each.
2. **Filter before presenting.** Apply steps 4 through 9 yourself. Bringing back thirty
   unchecked names is not help.
3. **Present a short list with reasoning attached**, grouped by territory, each with what
   it means, what it evokes, and what it rules out. Say which checks are clear and which
   still need a professional.
4. **Never present a name as legally cleared.** We can search; we cannot clear. Say so
   plainly, and recommend a trademark attorney before anything is registered or printed.
5. **Capture the reasoning as brief element 3** — the winner's *why* is the richest source
   of symbol ideas in the entire process, and it is lost if only the name is recorded.

## Search the name against existing identities too

One Behance query for `knife brand identity` surfaced *"Nesal"* — a published knife
identity under a near-identical name. **Visual collision and trademark collision are
different problems**, and this one is cheap to catch now and expensive to discover after
the mark is drawn.
