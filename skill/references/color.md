## The 60 / 30 / 10 rule

**Design with three colors.**

| Share | Role |
|-------|------|
| 60% | Primary |
| 30% | Secondary |
| 10% | Accent |

**This is a rule about area, not importance.** The 60% is the color the eye spends most
of its time on, which usually means the calmest one — a deep neutral, a paper tone, a
charcoal. The 10% is where the eye *goes*. A palette fails most often by making the
loudest color the 60%, which is exhausting to look at and leaves the accent nowhere to
go.

Jamrah, for reference: charcoal `#232323`, ember `#e85f1a`, cream `#f5e1cd`.

## Choosing the primary

The primary is derived, never picked because it looks nice. Go back to the root of the
identity and collect the answers already gathered:

- Who is the target audience
- What feelings and emotions the brand wants to deliver
- The added value of the company
- **The competitor study** — the differentiation method
- What makes this brand unique in the market
- The brand character

Take those answers to the color meanings below, and find the color that aligns with them.
That is the primary.

**The competitor method belongs here.** Deciding the primary color is the moment
differentiation is actually spent. Everything the competitor audit found — the occupied
hues, the shared register — applies at this exact step.

### Two warnings that matter more than the table

**Meaning is cultural, and this table is one culture's reading.** Red is love in the USA,
luck in China, mourning in South Africa, purity and fertility at Indian weddings. White is
purity in the West and mourning across much of Asia. Green carries religious and natural
weight in the Arab world that it does not carry elsewhere. **Always read the table against
the stated audience**, never as universal fact. Al-Nissal's own brief killed red — not
because red means danger everywhere, but because that brand refuses any association with
blood and weapons.

**The table is where everyone starts, which is why everyone ends up in the same place.**
Follow it mechanically and a bank gets blue, a health brand gets green, a knife brand gets
black and steel — alongside every competitor who read the same table. The table gives the
category convention. **The competitor audit decides whether to take it or break it.** Those
two inputs must be used together; the table alone produces the generic result.

## Choosing the secondary and the accent

Back to the color wheel — and not by feel. There are rules, and five standard methods.

Tool: **Adobe Color** — https://color.adobe.com/create/color-wheel. Strong for running
trials. **If it asks for a login, ask the user to sign in themselves.**

### The five harmony methods

#### Monochromatic

One hue, varied by lightness and saturation. Every color on the palette is the same
color at different strengths.

Calm, cohesive, effortlessly consistent, and impossible to make clash. The risk is
flatness — with no hue contrast, hierarchy has to come entirely from value, and low-value
contrast turns to mush. Strongest when a brand owns a single color outright.

PayPal — light blue, blue, navy. Animal Planet — three greens.

#### Complementary

Two hues opposite each other on the wheel.

Maximum contrast and maximum energy. Each color makes the other look more intense. That
is the strength and the danger: at equal saturation and equal area, complements vibrate
and fight. **60/30/10 is what makes this method usable** — give one hue dominance and let
the other appear only as accent.

LA Lakers — purple and gold. Tide — blue and orange.

#### Analogous

Three hues adjacent on the wheel.

Harmonious and natural — this is how light behaves in the physical world, which is why
sunsets and foliage look right. Comfortable, easy, low-conflict. The weakness is low
contrast: neighbors do not separate well, so one must be pushed in value or saturation to
create hierarchy.

BP — green, lime, yellow. Firefox — red, yellow, purple.

#### Triadic

Three hues evenly spaced around the wheel, 120° apart.

Vivid and balanced at once — contrast without the head-on collision of complements. Reads
as playful, energetic, confident, which is why food and entertainment brands reach for it.
Demanding to control: three strong hues need one clear lead or the result is a carnival.

Fanta — green, orange, blue. Burger King — red, yellow, blue.

#### Tetradic

Four hues in two complementary pairs — a rectangle or square on the wheel.

The richest and the hardest. Four hues give enormous range, and almost guarantee chaos
without strict discipline: one dominant, the rest strictly subordinate, and careful
attention to warm-cool balance since the scheme carries two of each. Rarely the right
answer for a small brand, and usually a sign a palette is trying to do too much.

### Choosing the accent

Same methods. The accent is the 10% — the color that appears least and is noticed most.
Reserve it for what should be looked at: calls to action, highlights, the one thing on the
page that matters.

### Contrast is not optional

Whatever the harmony, the palette must still hold text legibly. Check every foreground and
background pairing that will actually occur, and account for color blindness — a
red-and-green scheme that carries meaning is invisible to a meaningful share of people.
Harmony that cannot be read is a failed palette.

## Screen versus paper — RGB and CMYK

Colors behave differently on a screen than on paper, and the gap is not small.

**RGB is light; CMYK is ink.** Screens emit, paper reflects. Many bright RGB colors — the
electric blues, the neon greens, the saturated oranges — simply do not exist in CMYK and
print dull. If a brand will be printed at all, this must be checked before the palette is
approved, not after the packaging arrives wrong.

**Specify every color in four systems: HEX, RGB, CMYK, and PMS.** Each exists for a
different job.

| Code | Exists for |
|------|-----------|
| HEX | Web and digital handoff |
| RGB | Screen — emitted light |
| CMYK | Process printing, four-color |
| PMS | Spot color — exact, repeatable match |

**PMS is the one that gets dropped, and it should not be.** CMYK is mixed on press, so it
drifts between print runs, papers, and suppliers. Pantone is pre-mixed ink: the same color
from every supplier, in every country, every year. Packaging, signage, and merchandise
depend on it.

A complete swatch specification looks like: `HEX FEE75C` · `RGB 254, 231, 92` ·
`CMYK 0, 5, 80, 0` · `PMS 102 C`.

## The process is recursive, and the discipline is the point

Once a direction is set — say the primary will be black — inspiration can be gathered from
Pinterest or Behance to see how others handled it.

**Do not be moved easily by what is found there.** Reference is gathered to inform a
decision that was already derived from the brief, the audience, and the competitor study.
An attractive palette on Behance was derived from someone else's brief. Ours is the one
that governs.

Follow the rules.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing by personal preference — the founder's favorite color is not a method.
- Reading the meanings table as universal rather than cultural.
- Following the table without the competitor audit, and landing exactly where the
  category already is.
- Making the loudest color the 60%.
- Approving a palette on screen that was always going to be printed.
- Ignoring contrast and color blindness.
