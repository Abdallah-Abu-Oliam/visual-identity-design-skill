# Logo Types

Source: https://www.logodesign.net/blog/choose-the-right-logo-type/

The source lists ten. Seven are genuine types — mutually exclusive answers to "what kind
of mark is this." Three are **treatments**: ways of executing a type, not alternatives to
one. A negative-space logo is still a pictorial mark. A 3D logo is a rendering style. A
dynamic logo is a behavior. Keeping them separate matters, because otherwise a treatment
gets chosen *instead of* a type and the actual decision never happens.

---

## The seven types

### 1. Wordmark (logotype)

The brand name itself, drawn. Purely typographic, no imagery.

**Use when** the name is short, distinctive, or memorable · scalability matters ·
food and beverage, technology, legal, construction, finance.

**Avoid when** the name is long, generic, or forgettable — a wordmark has nothing to hide
behind, and everything rests on the typography.

Coca-Cola · Google · Uber

### 2. Lettermark (monogram)

Two or three initials in a custom style, nothing else.

**Use when** the full name is long, complicated, or hard to remember · a modern,
professional, minimal register is wanted.

**Avoid when** the initials are not distinctive, or the brand is unknown — initials mean
nothing until people already know what they stand for.

IBM · CNN · HBO

### 3. Pictorial mark (brandmark)

One strong, simple image. No text.

**Use when** the brand already has recognition · tech and retail · a clean minimal style
is wanted.

**Avoid when the business is new.** This is the source's sharpest rule and the most
commonly ignored. A symbol with no name attached only works once people can already name
it. New brands that choose this are buying a mark nobody can identify.

Apple · Target

### 4. Abstract mark

A unique geometric or constructed form that represents nothing recognizable, carrying
meaning through shape, color, and movement rather than depiction.

**Use when** differentiation from competitors is the priority · the offering evolves or
may change · lifestyle, design, tech, consulting.

**Avoid when** the audience needs to grasp the purpose immediately, or the strategy is
not strong enough to give the form meaning. An abstract mark means whatever the brand
puts into it — which is nothing, at first.

Airbnb · Spotify

### 5. Combination mark

A wordmark plus a symbol. Stacked, side by side, or fused into one shape.

**Use when** the brand is new — it builds name recognition and symbol recognition at the
same time, and the symbol can be split off later for apps, favicons, and co-branding once
people know it.

**Cost:** more complex to design, and it needs multiple locked layouts.

The most common choice, and usually the correct one for a brand starting out.

Adidas · Lacoste

### 6. Emblem

Text held inside a symbol — badge, crest, seal — as one indivisible image.

**Use when** heritage, tradition, craft, or authority is the point · education, sports,
luxury goods.

**Avoid when** small sizes and digital flexibility matter. Emblems carry detail, and
detail dies at favicon scale. Text locked inside the shape cannot be separated for
different layouts.

NFL · Porsche · Yale

### 7. Mascot

An illustrated character, figure, or animal.

**Use when** emotional connection and community are central · sports, food, restaurants,
education.

**Avoid when** the brand is premium, quiet, or serious. Also expensive to sustain — a
character needs ongoing illustration and dates faster than any other type.

KFC · Pillsbury · Duolingo

## The three treatments

Applied to a type, never chosen instead of one.

### Negative space

Empty space forms a second shape, letter, or meaning inside the mark.

Simple, scalable, memorable when it lands — and easy to miss entirely. Hard to execute.
Architecture, creative agencies, fashion, tech.

FedEx · NBC

### 3D

Lighting, perspective, shadow, gradient, and texture producing depth.

Strong impact on digital surfaces and packaging. Struggles at small sizes and in print,
and **always requires a flat variant**, which means the flat version is the real logo.

Xbox · Ford

### Dynamic

A consistent core that varies by context — color, pattern, layout, or motion.

Shows range and stays current. Expensive to manage, hard to keep consistent, and awkward
in print. Requires an already-solid fixed mark underneath.

Google Doodle · Spotify Wrapped

## Choosing

The source's four criteria for a mark that lasts: **memorability, simplicity,
versatility, relevance.**

Its process: consider the industry and review competitor logos first, define the brand
personality, match typography and visual style to that personality, check the connection
to the target audience, then test scalability across applications.

**How this connects to our process.** The type is not picked from a list of preferences —
it falls out of material already gathered:

| Input | What it decides |
|-------|-----------------|
| Name — long, short, distinctive, generic | Wordmark versus lettermark. A generic name cannot carry a wordmark |
| Brand age and existing recognition | Rules pictorial marks in or out. New brand → combination |
| Competitor audit (Step 3) | Which types are already saturated in the category |
| Personality and targeted feelings | Mascot versus emblem versus abstract |
| Required applications (Q13) | Emblems fail where small sizes and co-branding matter |
| Q14 | The user's stated preference — a constraint, not the decision |

**Q14 is an input to this, not a substitute for it.** Al-Nissal chose pictorial, wordmark,
and combination. The rules above resolve that: pictorial is wrong on its own for a brand
without existing recognition, and combination is right for exactly the reason it exists —
it builds both at once and decomposes into a standalone symbol later for engraving,
signage, and co-branding, all of which that brief requires.

When the rules and the user's preference disagree, say so and explain why. Do not
silently overrule, and do not silently comply.
